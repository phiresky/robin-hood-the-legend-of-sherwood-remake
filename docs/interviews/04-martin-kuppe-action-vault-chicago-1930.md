In October of 1919, the American federal government enacted the Volstead Act, a piece of legislation providing for enforcement of the Eighteenth Amendment, which banned "the manufacture, sale, or distribution of intoxicating liquors" - defined as beverages containing more than a heady 0.5 percent alcohol. Also known as the National Prohibition Act, it went into effect in July of the following year, giving authorities the right to prosecute violators. As foolish as all this might seem now, it was characterized by Herbert Hoover as a "noble experiment". The intent was to improve the population's health and reduce crime, which would also cut the cost of running the prison system. Of course, the lawmakers completely failed to take into account simple human nature, in particular the natural resistance people have to legal meddling in the ordinary affairs of their daily lives.

The prohibition era that followed serves as the inspiration for Chicago 1930, which is currently in progress at [Spellbound Studios](http://www.spellbound.de). Described as combining strategy, stealth and action elements, the game reportedly begins with the Mafia controlling large parts of the city. A special police task force has been formed in order to break its hold. At this point, the player's role and objective may seem pretty obvious, but the developers have thrown in a twist by allowing you to play either side. The gangster forces are charged with committing criminal acts such as murders and kidnappings, while the agents of the law must take back the community quarter by quarter by accomplishing missions like investigations. To learn more, we took Lead Game Designer and AI Programmer Martin Kuppe hostage for this Chicago 1930 Interview, Part 1.

* * *

**Action Vault: How would you summarize Chicago 1930? What's it about, what type of game is it, and what kind of gameplay should players expect? And what led to the choice of the location and time?

Martin Kuppe:** Chicago 1930 is a real-time strategy game with infiltration tactics and combat action, just like Desperados or Robin Hood - I repeated this sentence 256755874 times at our stand at the E3, so I know it by heart. :o) The player has the choice between playing the good guys (FBI) or the bad guys (Mafia), with quite different gameplay for both sides.

We have chosen a Mafia setting because it is an interesting universe that has not yet used very often in team-based tactical games. Why we have chosen Chicago? Well, you know, we hesitated between Chicago and Honolulu, but we finally realized that in Honolulu, they never had many problems with the Mafia... Why 1930? Because in 1840, the streets of Chicago were quite peaceful. ;o) No, seriously, the Windy City during the prohibition is the ideal setting for a Mafia game.

**Action Vault: What more are you willing to say about the setting, the campaigns for the opposing sides, the differences between them, and the extent to which they are related to each other?

Martin Kuppe:** The game takes place in 1930 in Chicago. :o))) More exactly, the story starts in 1928 when Don Falcone arrives at Chicago. On the Mafia side, the player takes the role of Jack Beretto, the right arm of Don Falcone. The campaign consists of taking the city quarter after quarter, up to the final mission in 1930, where the objective is to kill the District Attorney in his own apartment.

The police campaign starts with the investigation of a murder. The district attorney has been murdered in his apartment... Here, the player is Edouard Nash, the chief of a group of federal agents whose task is to fight against the mob. The goal of the police campaign is to drive the Mafia out of the city and take Chicago back.

There is a storyline linking the two campaigns. In the Mafia campaign, the player learns that there is a mysterious "Baron" who secretly removes the stitches in the Falcone empire. In the police campaign, the quest continues, we find new evidence about the Baron... In the final mission, we learn who the man behind the curtain really was.

The player can also start with the police campaign and then play the Mafia campaign as a flashback. In any case he may learn the truth about the Baron before finishing the two campaigns.

**Action Vault: In addition to the police and criminal sides having separate campaigns, to what extent should we look for other elements of the gameplay to differ?

Martin Kuppe:** The game is quite different for the Mafia and for the police. Of course there are different mission goals. As a gangster, you have to kill enemy gangsters, kidnap a district attorney, destroy the beer reserves of a rival gang, assassinate a judge, etc. As a cop, you have to investigate on murders, free hostages, make police raids etc.

But it is not only about different mission goals and different suits. The gameplay is quite different. As a gangster, you can kill whoever you want - you just have to take care of the witnesses afterwards, either by killing them or by bribing them. As a cop, you don't have the right at all to commit crimes, even if nobody is looking. Above all, you are not allowed to kill people, except in cases of self-defence. When you break these rules, the guilty characters will go to jail after the mission.

Furthermore as a cop, you cannot arrest a gangster because you think he is a gangster. If you want to arrest someone, you must first follow legal steps by finding evidence and interrogating witnesses.

In general, the police gameplay is much more about infiltration, looking for evidence, interrogating witnesses, then arresting the gangsters, and shooting only when you are attacked. The Mafia gameplay is more brutal - which does not necessarily mean that you can shoot anyone and anything around with your Tommy gun from the beginning, because if you do so, you will be surrounded by a dozen cops within three seconds.

**Action Vault: On a more detailed level, what range of locations does Chicago 1930 include? How much research did you do in this regard?

Martin Kuppe:** There are eight different locations in the game, some of them consisting of two floors - a luxury hotel, a factory, a brothel, a restaurant, a train station, a harbor zone, a motel and an apartment.

Our artists did a lot of research about architecture, furniture, fashion, etc. during the prohibition era. The locations are partially inspired by both real locations and film settings - for example, the railroad station which is an allusion to the famous railroad station scene in the movie The Untouchables - including the baby carriage.

**Action Vault: As for the structure of the respective campaigns, how do they begin, and from there, do they continue in a linear progression or is there some degree of player choice?

Martin Kuppe:** You choose the next mission on the map of Chicago. Take the Mafia campaign for an example. When you start the game, you see the map of Chicago - eight quarters, corresponding to the eight locations. Five of them are colored brown, which means that they are owned by a rival gang. The remaining three are blue; they are controlled by the authorities. One of the brown quarters to the west is blinking - this is your first mission. You click on it, receive the briefing, compose your team, equip it and start the mission.

After the mission, the quarter is colored in red, which is your color, and two of the adjacent brown quarters are blinking. You can choose where to attack, and so on... This way, the player conquers the city quarter by quarter. The police campaign goes the other way round - the whole map is in red, and you have to re-paint it in blue... Thus we have two non-linear campaigns consisting of 10 missions each (eight missions to conquer the quarters and two additional missions for the storyline).

**Action Vault: Since we know the game is squad-based, how many characters does the player actually control at any one time, and when and how are its members selected?

Martin Kuppe:** As noted before, in the Mafia campaign the player character is Jack Beretto, the right-hand man of Don Falcone. In the police campaign, the player takes the role of federal agent Edouard Nash, chief of a group of untouchable agents in charge of the fight against the Mafia. The mission team always consists of the main character and up to four other characters that can be chosen freely before each mission. There is a pool of up to 15 different characters per campaign, each one having his own attribute values.

**Action Vault: What are these attributes you mentioned, and is there an advancement system that allows characters to improve in them during the course of the game?

Martin Kuppe:** There are five attributes - shooting, close combat, throwing, medicine and charisma. Furthermore, the characters may have special bonuses for some actions, for example "Tommy gun +2". The characters can train the attributes by using them - when you use, for example, grenades and throwing knives very often, your throwing attribute gets better.

You can also train your characters in the sports club once you have conquered the quarter with the "sports club" icon. This is an example of the resources placed in the Chicago map. There are the following resources: speakeasies (where you can get new men), armories (where you get access to new weapons and equipment), sports club (to train your men), tribunal (to reduce the time your characters are in jail) and newspaper (to increase popularity).

**Action Vault: How does the combat system work, what are its important features, and what is the balance between direct combat and other types of gameplay such as the obvious one, stealth?

Martin Kuppe:** The combat system is based on an intuitive point and click interface. You click on a weapon in your inventory, and then click on the adversary to use it. When it is a firearm, we will have a recoil effect on the cursor, depending on the weapon and on the character's shooting ability. Some weapons need a bit more consideration, for example, the pineapple grenade. When you have selected the grenade, you press the left mouse button down to pull the ring, and you release it to throw the grenade. It's all about timing; when you toss it too early, the adversaries will duck and take cover, or even pick up the grenade and throw it back. If you hold it longer, it may explode in your hand, which is not really a good idea. But we have some graphical help for the player. A countdown is displayed over the grenade, and you see the exact trajectory it will take.

As a general remark, you only see what your characters see. When you have seen an enemy and he leaves your 180-degree view area, he slowly fades out. When your characters hear an NPC, this is displayed as a "blip" consisting of concentric circles.

As for the balance, we have maybe 60% stealth, 40% direct combat. In general, you start a mission with stealth tactics, as they are full of adversaries. When you have cleaned up the level a bit, you can dare to use noisy weapons like guns and grenades. Often, the level ends with a big final gunfight.

**Action Vault: What kind of pace are you aiming for in the stealth and combat aspects? How will the slow-motion mode work, and will it be possible to pause the action completely to issue orders?

Martin Kuppe:** As long as you use stealth tactics, the game is relatively slow. Of course, you sometimes have to react fast; for example, when you see or hear a guard coming around the corner. When it comes to combat, it is, of course, much faster. In fact, in a big gunfight, it may become too fast to handle - therefore, we have the slow motion mode.

With a click on an icon, the game is slowed down by the factor of five. Graphically we have an "old film effect" and motion blur effects on the characters; you can see bullets flying through the air... We hear a heartbeat sound, and the in-game sounds are sampled down. In the slow motion mode, you have enough time to give orders to different characters, react instantly to enemy behavior and so on. Furthermore, you can in this mode assign targets to your characters. One will then shoot at the cop on the left until he is dead, another the cop on the right... The assigned targets are represented by dotted lines; this way, you see which characters have a target and which ones are waiting for new orders.

And, no, we have no pause mode where you give orders - if we would have a pause, nobody would use our nice slow motion mode. ;o)

**While Chicago 1930 doesn't have a pause mode, we do - and it's rather useful in instances where developers are willing to provide enough information to span multiple interview segments. As you can see, this is the case here and now. Accordingly, we'll take a break before returning with more from Martin Kuppe on Spellbound's game, which should happen in the not to distant future. And for now, of course, we thank him for what we have learned so far.

* * *

**Action Vault: During the course of the game, what assortment of weapons will be available to the player, and to what extent are they accurate in representing the actual arms of the period?

Martin Kuppe:** We have four kinds of firearms (Colt .38, Winchester, pump gun and Tommy gun), four kinds of close combat weapon (knuckleduster, baseball bat, baton and switchblade knife) and some other weapons (pineapple grenade, throwing knife, chloroform). The more weapons the player owns, the more weapons he has at his disposition. The weapon characteristics are adapted to the gameplay, not to historical facts.

**Action Vault: What were your primary objectives in designing and implementing the enemy NPCs, especially with respect to the artificial intelligence that controls their behavior?

Martin Kuppe:** The main goal was to have enemies with realistic and "human-like" behavior. Enemy NPCs react differently when they see player characters, depending on the situation. A gangster drinking his milk shake at the hotel bar will, for example, not react in the same way as a guard in front of a door, a lookout on a street corner, a cop patrolling in the street or a gangster counting money in a back room. Furthermore, the NPCs should fight in an intelligent and cooperative way - look for help, encircle the player characters, hide behind desks, choose optimal shooting position, etc.

**Action Vault: Will there be many friendly NPCs, or neutral ones like innocent bystanders? What kinds of roles will they play, and how important will they be overall?

Martin Kuppe:** The civilians play an important role in the gameplay. In the police campaign, civilian witnesses are your main source of information. In the Mafia campaign, civilians may become witnesses to your crimes. When a civilian sees one of your characters committing a murder, she becomes a witness - which is indicated by an eye symbol above her head. When you don't take care of the witness, she will tell everything to the police after the mission, and maybe your gangster will go to jail. So, better take care of the witnesses. The simplest way is to kill them - but there is a little problem. Imagine you commit a murder. There is a chambermaid who sees what you did and who runs into the restaurant. Well, you follow her and kill her in public. *bang* But eight of the 20 guests in the restaurant see you - eight more witnesses to kill... *bang, bang, bang...* Now the remaining 12 guests are also witnesses. *Bang, bang, bang, bang...* Oops, the waiter has come out of the kitchen. *Bang*. Just at this moment, five other guests come in...

In the end, you have no more surviving witnesses, and nobody will go to jail - but there is a big heap of dead witnesses in the restaurant. And a big heap of dead bodies in Chicago during the prohibition - everybody knows that this was not the Ku Klux Klan... So, if you kill all the witnesses, the popularity of the Mafia will drop, and you will get less new men. But there is another way to take care of the witnesses. Bribe them! So, if you have some bribe money in your pockets, talk with the chambermaid, give her some bills, and she has seen nothing.

**Action Vault: How many missions will there be for each side, and to what degree have you designed them to differ in terms of the gameplay and goals?

Martin Kuppe:** There will be overall 20 missions in the game, 10 for each campaign. They provide a wide variety of mission objectives. As a gangster you have, for example, to kill a judge, to destroy the beer reserves of a rival gang, to assassinate enemy gangsters, to kidnap a district attorney or to help Don Falcone out of a police raid. As a fed, you make raids, investigate murders, search for weapons in a secret weapon stock, protect a witness, free hostages, etc.

**Action Vault: It seems the game is single-player only? Is this correct, and why did you decide not to have multiplayer?

Martin Kuppe:** It's only single-player. The gameplay does not match very well with a multiplayer mode. Initially, we discussed a lot about whether a multiplayer mode is possible, but finally decided to focus on single-player.

**Action Vault: What engine are you using, and which of its features are especially important? What do you think the system requirements and recommendations for Chicago 1930 will be?

Martin Kuppe:** The engine is based on our own Desperados / Robin Hood engine, but with some improvements. First, the sprites are lighted dynamically. When a character goes into a shadow zone, he gets darker; when he approaches a light, he gets brighter. Second, we have particle effects on all kinds of broken objects - lamps, vases, plants... Furthermore, we have a sprite re-coloring system that allows having an individual sprite for each of the 30 available player characters.

As for the system requirements, the game should run on a PII 300 with 64 MB RAM and a graphics card with 8 MB.

**Action Vault: When did the idea for the game arise, and when did development begin? What is the present status, when will it be ready for release, and in which markets are you planning to release it?

Martin Kuppe:** In 2002, we discussed what kind of game we could create based on the Robin Hood engine, and finally, we had the idea that a Mafia game would be great. We started work in Q4 2002, and we are just before the alpha version. In Europe, the game will be in stores before Christmas 2003, except if an asteroid crashes down on our company. ;o)

**Action Vault: What factors will make Chicago 1930 stand out, and what kind of gamer do you see as your core target market? Approximately what duration of gameplay are you aiming for, and what level of replayability do you anticipate?

Martin Kuppe:** As far as I know, Chicago 1930 is the only infiltration tactics game where you can choose between the good guys and the bad guys. Other strong points are the RPG aspect, the background story and the slow motion mode, which transforms messy gunfights into tactical challenges.

The game will have three degrees of difficulty, so occasional players and hardcore gamers can enjoy it. The core target group is gamers who like infiltration and action games such as Commandos, Desperados and Robin Hood.
The duration of gameplay is very difficult to estimate at the current state, but I think it will be around 10 hours of gameplay. The RPG component and the non-linear campaigns will give a strong motivation to replay the game.

**Action Vault: Please tell our readers a bit about Spellbound and its history. Where is the studio located and who are the core members of the Chicago 1930 team? What experience do you have in game development?

Martin Kuppe:** Spellbound was founded in 1995 and has developed the games Perry Rhodan: Operation Eastside, Airline Tycoon, Desperados and Robin Hood. The studio is located at Kehl, a small German town just at the French border. We just have to cross the Rhine bridge, and we are in Strassbourg. Most of the team members are French.

The key members of our development team are project manager Jean-Michel Stenger, technical director Stéphane Becker, lead programmer Arno Wienholtz, lead level designer Mickael Parisot, and last but not least art director Jean-Marc Haessig - all French people. Oh, and also me. Personally, I am German, but being surrounded by French people, I speak French all day long... But there are over a dozen people working on this project, and every single one plays an important role. Most of them are on the team since the development of Robin Hood or even of Desperados, and some even longer.

**Action Vault: Are there any other interesting features or elements you'd like to discuss? Is there anything else you'd like to tell our readers, or something you'd like to ask them?

Martin Kuppe:** I just want to say that our artists did (and do) a great job with the level graphics, as they did in Desperados and Robin Hood. The levels are like big paintings, with great ambience and lots of little details, and sometimes (but don't tell this to my boss!) I spend 10 minutes just looking a level background and enjoying the graphics!

**Thousands of gamers around the world have enjoyed the graphics and more importantly the gameplay in Desperados, Robin Hood - The Legend of Sherwood and the other titles that have been released to date by Spellbound Studios. We join them in looking forward to the arrival of Chicago 1930 late this year in Europe, and perhaps at a date to be determined in North America as well. In the meantime, we thank Martin Kuppe for answering these questions to complete our two-part interview.**
