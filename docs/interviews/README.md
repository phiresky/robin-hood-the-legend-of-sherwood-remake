# Robin Hood / Spellbound interview archive

This index covers 18 distinct interviews represented by 24 preserved source pages. Each linked Markdown file contains one complete interview in its original language; multipart web features are kept together in reading order. The English summaries below are editorial descriptions written for this archive, not translations or source text. Navigation, advertising, comments, and unrelated page furniture are omitted from the transcripts, while the untouched HTML or PDF source files remain available under [`sources/`](sources/).

Languages represented: German and English.

## 1. [Daniel Würstl on Chicago 1930 and Desperados 2](01-daniel-wuerstl.md)

- Interviewee(s): Daniel Würstl
- Publication: robinhood-game.de
- Original language: German
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: Chicago 1930 design and release plans; Desperados 2 early development; transition from isometric 2D to full 3D; character progression and nonlinear missions; level-editor and modding obstacles

Spellbound marketing employee Daniel Würstl discusses the studio’s projects and plans around 2003. He outlines Chicago 1930’s anticipated Christmas release, upgraded isometric graphics, predominantly indoor settings, nonlinear mission selection, territory-based progression, character development, and flexible team composition. He then addresses the early development of Desperados 2: Cooper’s Revenge, explaining that its projected late-2004 release was uncertain, a publisher presentation demo was underway, and the team had chosen full 3D partly to move beyond its older engine. He describes a variable camera, returning and new characters, and an intent to preserve the original game’s atmosphere, while stressing that its story and many details remained unsettled. Würstl also comments on music, the practical obstacles to releasing a level editor, and Spellbound’s promotional presence at Games Convention, while declining to disclose several commercial and narrative details.

## 2. [Tapio Muley on composing Robin Hood](02-tapio-muley.md)

- Interviewee(s): Tapio Muley
- Publication: robinhood-game.de
- Original language: German
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: soundtrack and sound-effect production; collaboration with Spellbound; level artwork as musical inspiration; electronic composition equipment; advice for aspiring game composers

Composer Tapio Muley describes creating the in-game music and contributing sound effects for Robin Hood: The Legend of Sherwood, his first soundtrack for a commercially released game of this scale. Working freelance alongside his job as a network administrator, he collaborated closely with Spellbound through both email exchanges and frequent on-site discussions. Muley explains that the team chose a musical approach combining cinematic and medieval elements to reinforce the game’s atmosphere. He used large printed images of each level beside his keyboard as visual inspiration, giving individual locations their own musical character. Although deadlines and technical limitations prevented some ideas from being realized, he was proud of the result. He also discusses his electronic studio setup and advises aspiring game composers to develop creativity, initiative, practical experience, and a portfolio through freeware projects and demos.

## 3. [Mischa Strecker on Robin Hood’s development](03-mischa-strecker-robinhood-game.md)

- Interviewee(s): Mischa Strecker
- Publication: robinhood-game.de
- Original language: German
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: relationship to Desperados; engine and animation improvements; reworked enemy AI; difficulty and mission preparation; playable characters, weapons, and ransom economy

Spellbound press and community manager Mischa Strecker discusses the development of Robin Hood: The Legend of Sherwood and its relationship to Desperados. He rejects the claim that Desperados was merely a technical rehearsal, while acknowledging that its development experience informed the newer game. Strecker describes engine improvements enabling larger image data, smoother animation, and more detailed characters, alongside a substantially rewritten AI system featuring formations, officer commands, and situational combat decisions. He also outlines three difficulty levels, optional preparatory missions, a varied medieval arsenal, and nine playable characters whose mission composition can be chosen by the player. Money serves the narrative objective of paying Richard the Lionheart’s ransom. Finally, he attributes confidence in the planned release schedule to improved project planning and accumulated production experience, but offers no new information about the proposed Desperados webisodes or Airline Tycoon Evolution.

## 4. [Martin Kuppe on Chicago 1930 — Action Vault](04-martin-kuppe-action-vault-chicago-1930.md)

- Interviewee(s): Martin Kuppe
- Publication: Action Vault
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 3
- Key topics: dual Mafia and federal-agent campaigns; stealth, witnesses, and law enforcement; squad progression and district conquest; slow-motion combat and AI; reused and enhanced Robin Hood engine

Spellbound lead game designer and AI programmer Martin Kuppe explains the design of Chicago 1930, a squad-based tactics game built on the Desperados and Robin Hood engine. Its linked Mafia and federal-agent campaigns approach the same struggle from opposing sides, with distinct rules for crime, evidence, witnesses, arrests, and lethal force. Kuppe describes the non-linear district-conquest structure, character selection and advancement, strategic city resources, historically inspired locations, and a combat system balanced toward stealth. He also discusses intelligent NPC behavior, civilians who can report or be bribed, and slow motion as a tactical substitute for pausing. The concluding section covers weapons, missions, engine improvements such as dynamic sprite lighting, production history, Spellbound’s Franco-German development team, expected requirements, replayability, and the studio’s emphasis on richly detailed painted environments.

## 5. [Armin Gessert on Chicago 1930 — WorthPlaying](05-armin-gessert-worthplaying-chicago-1930.md)

- Interviewee(s): Armin Gessert
- Publication: WorthPlaying
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: Chicago 1930 concept and design; historical research and missions; resources and character progression; AI and stealth systems; comparison with Robin Hood

This short E3-era Q&A with Spellbound founder Armin Gessert introduces Chicago 1930 and explains how Wanadoo selected Prohibition-era organized crime as a setting for Spellbound’s tactical gameplay. Gessert describes the historical research behind the game, its parallel Mafia and federal-agent campaigns, varied mission objectives, and a resource system that unlocks equipment, recruits, upgrades, and other advantages. The interview is promotional in character but provides a concise account of how Spellbound intended to evolve its established infiltration-and-combat formula.

## 6. [Martin Kuppe on Al Capone / Chicago 1930 — HomeLAN Fed](06-martin-kuppe-homelan-al-capone.md)

- Interviewee(s): Martin Kuppe
- Publication: HomeLAN Fed
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: Chicago 1930 development; dual Mafia and police campaigns; tactical stealth gameplay; Robin Hood engine evolution; historical research

Spellbound game designer Martin Kuppe introduces Al Capone, later released as Chicago 1930, and explains how lessons from Robin Hood: The Legend of Sherwood shaped its design. Set during Prohibition, the game presents interconnected Mafia and police campaigns starring Frank Nitti and Eliot Ness, with historically researched characters, architecture, and events. Kuppe describes a more cautious, tactically balanced evolution of Robin Hood’s team-based real-time strategy: restricted visibility, shadows, cover, ammunition management, witnesses, evidence, arrests, and consequences for unlawful violence distinguish the two factions. He also discusses RPG-style character development, inventories, period weapons, slow motion, and enhancements to the Robin Hood engine such as sprite recoloring and adaptive illumination. The interview closes with the project’s production status, absence of multiplayer, intended accessibility, and the indefinite shelving of Spellbound’s Vampire Hunter project.

## 7. [Jean-Marc Haessig on Robin Hood’s visual design — Action Vault Q&A](07-jean-marc-haessig-action-vault-qa.md)

- Interviewee(s): Jean-Marc Haessig
- Publication: Action Vault
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 2
- Key topics: art direction and visual influences; historical research versus gameplay; enhanced Desperados engine; sprite animation and detailed 2D graphics; production constraints and teamwork

Creative director Jean-Marc Haessig explains the visual direction and technology behind Robin Hood: The Legend of Sherwood. Spellbound sought a colorful, playful synthesis of familiar Robin Hood portrayals, drawing particularly on Technicolor adventure films and Disney while exaggerating character proportions, animation, architecture, and color. Historical research informed the setting, but material was selectively adapted to suit gameplay and production needs. Haessig describes the team’s goals of emotional detail, distinctive locations, genre innovation, and a respectful but flexible treatment of the legend. He also discusses practical constraints, the unusually accurate production schedule, and improvements to the Desperados engine. Its compressed, hand-detailed 2D backgrounds and enlarged sprite animations enabled rich visuals, zooming, and broad support for contemporary PCs, although they could not benefit much from increasingly 3D-oriented graphics hardware. He credits collaborative teamwork for exceeding the project’s initial artistic expectations.

## 8. [Robin Hood staff favorite-character Q&A — Action Vault](08-action-vault-favorite-character-qa.md)

- Publication: Action Vault
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: playable-character roster; staff character preferences; character-specific abilities; non-lethal combat and crowd control; Robin Hood characterization

This Action Vault feature is a multi-participant favorite-character Q&A rather than a conventional single-subject interview. It opens with the game’s political premise and brief profiles of its six principal playable characters: Robin Hood, Little John, Maid Marian, Will Scarlet, Friar Tuck, and Stuteley. The piece provides a compact preview of character differentiation, combat moves, traversal skills, and the game’s playful treatment of the Robin Hood legend.

## 9. [Martin Kuppe on Robin Hood — XGR](09-martin-kuppe-xgr.md)

- Interviewee(s): Martin Kuppe
- Publication: XGR
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 2
- Key topics: story and Robin Hood traditions; nonlinear missions and Sherwood management; team abilities and Quickactions; stealth, combat, and non-lethal play; 2D engine and animation technology

Spellbound game designer and AI programmer Martin Kuppe explains how Robin Hood adapts the legend into a nonlinear, team-based infiltration game descended from Desperados. He outlines the political story, its blend of medieval ballads and later traditions, and the challenge of portraying familiar characters. The interview details 37 missions across eight reusable locations, Sherwood headquarters management, convoy ambushes, castle battles, and incentives for sparing enemies. Kuppe describes character-specific abilities, weapons, coordinated Quickactions, and a choice between stealth and gesture-driven sword fighting. On technology, he discusses Spellbound’s internally developed 2D engine, painted backgrounds, underlying 3D geometry, directional sprites, and smooth animation transitions. He also confirms a forthcoming one-mission demo, gives the planned November 2002 release, and briefly discusses his role, anticipated games, and personal interests.

Creative director Jean-Marc Haessig and Wanadoo producer Anne Devouassoux present Robin Hood: The Legend of Sherwood as a real-time tactics game designed to broaden the genre beyond its familiar wartime settings. They explain its non-linear campaign, which mixes story missions with optional ambushes, sieges, rescues, spying, and resource-gathering activities centered on funding King Richard’s ransom. ## 12. [Jean-Marc Haessig on Robin Hood — Computer and Video Games](12-jean-marc-haessig-cvg.md)

- Interviewee(s): Jean-Marc Haessig
- Publication: Computer and Video Games
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: real-time tactics and mission structure; stealth versus direct combat; squad composition and character abilities; historical research and adapted locations; technical, combat, and AI advances over Desperados

Creative director Jean-Marc Haessig presents Robin Hood as a mission-based real-time tactics game that adapts the familiar outlaw legend as an alternative to the genre’s prevalent wartime settings. Players assemble squads of up to five characters, gather ransom money for King Richard, and choose among story and repeatable random missions across Sherwood and several English towns and castles. Haessig emphasizes player choice between stealth and direct combat, character-specific abilities, interactive traps, and a mouse-gesture melee system. He also describes Spellbound’s historical and visual research, including medieval books, films, maps, and architecture, while noting that authentic locations were modified for gameplay. Compared with Desperados, the game introduces larger, more detailed animations, amulets, expanded combat mechanics, and less script-dependent AI whose guards respond according to their abilities and circumstances.

- Publication: Freelancer / AG.ru
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: evolved Desperados technology; non-linear campaign structure; stealth-versus-combat choice; enhanced AI and quick actions; Robin Hood adaptations and humor

Mischa Strecker describes Robin Hood: The Legend of Sherwood as an evolution of Desperados, built on a substantially revised engine with rewritten AI, larger graphics and animations, and a player-controlled hand-to-hand combat system. - Publication: Krawall
- Original language: German
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: Spellbound and Desperados; historical and cinematic influences; mission and gameplay design; player choice and game balance; Wanadoo publishing partnership

Spellbound’s Mischa Strecker discusses the studio’s transition from Desperados to Robin Hood: The Legend of Sherwood. He describes Desperados as a respectable commercial success, while confirming that no add-on was in development. Work on Robin Hood began in October 2001 after Wanadoo and Spellbound independently arrived at compatible ideas for the property. Strecker outlines varied missions involving infiltration, rescues, ambushes, traps, and castle sieges, alongside improved AI, direct mouse-controlled duels, character progression, resource management, and flexible lethal or non-lethal approaches. He identifies balancing the player’s freedom as the main development challenge. The interview also explains the game’s relationship to Commandos, rejects multiplayer as unsuitable for its design, and discusses Spellbound’s broader ambitions and publishing partnership with Wanadoo.

## 16. [Martin Kuppe on Robin Hood — WorthPlaying](16-martin-kuppe-worthplaying.md)

- Publication: WorthPlaying
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: tactical gameplay; character and resource management; 2D presentation and 3D simulation; replayability and progression; development and release status

Martin Kuppe, co-designer and AI programmer, explains how Spellbound adapted its Desperados engine to the Robin Hood legend and expanded the team-based tactical formula. He describes selecting up to five characters, managing training and ammunition in Sherwood, and approaching missions through stealth, combat, archery, ambush traps, and optional objectives. ## 17. [Willem Drijver on porting Robin Hood to Apollo Vampire V4](17-willem-drijver-whatiff.md)

- Interviewee(s): Willem Drijver
- Publication: WhatIFF? Issue 3.13
- Original language: English
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: Apollo Vampire V4 port; performance optimization; 68080 and SAGA architecture; Amiga development; prospective Desperados port

Willem Drijver discusses his background in computer science, the Atari and Amiga retail business, and his later involvement with Apollo hardware and software. He explains that porting Robin Hood: The Legend of Sherwood to Apollo Vampire V4 began as a personal experiment to study professional game development. The game’s detailed visuals and atmosphere motivated him to adapt and optimize it for Amiga-style hardware. The principal challenge is achieving stable performance on the 68080 CPU and SAGA chipset, which has required rewriting critical graphics, audio, and other routines in C++ and Apollo-specific assembly. At the time of the interview, he considers the port to be in beta, although testing remains limited to the demo level and no release date is promised. He also mentions possible future work involving Desperados, ApolloBoot, and ApolloOS.

## 18. [Fabian Del Priore on composing Chicago 1930](18-fabian-del-priore-chicago-1930.md)

- Interviewee(s): Fabian Del Priore
- Publication: chicago1930.de.vu
- Original language: German, with a brief English closing remark
- Preserved source pages: 1
- Key topics: Chicago 1930 soundtrack production; musical inspiration and composing process; Tapio Muley’s Robin Hood score; referral to and collaboration with Spellbound; early game-music career

Fabian Del Priore explains how sending a demo tape to Chris Hülsbeck in 1995 led to his career composing music for computer games. The interview primarily concerns his work on Chicago 1930: he composed all of its music, contributed sound effects, and scored its rendered sequences, intro, outro, and mission jingles. He describes drawing inspiration from Spellbound-provided screenshots, cutscenes, reference music, development builds, and the game’s dark gangster atmosphere. Del Priore had played Robin Hood: The Legend of Sherwood and Desperados while preparing for the project. He praises Tapio Muley’s Robin Hood score and explains that Muley, familiar with his earlier work, recommended him to Spellbound. Although they had not yet met personally, they were connected through the studio. Del Priore characterizes Spellbound as an experienced, friendly team and expresses interest in working with them again.
