Chicago 1930 Interview, Part 2
 August 18 2003


In October of 1919, the American federal government enacted the Volstead Act, a piece of legislation providing for enforcement of the Eighteenth Amendment, which banned "the manufacture, sale, or distribution of intoxicating liquors" - defined as beverages containing more than a heady 0.5 percent alcohol. Also known as the National Prohibition Act, it went into effect in July of the following year, giving authorities the right to prosecute violators. As foolish as all this might seem now, it was characterized by Herbert Hoover as a "noble experiment". The intent was to improve the population's health and reduce crime, which would also cut the cost of running the prison system. Of course, the lawmakers completely failed to take into account simple human nature, in particular the natural resistance people have to legal meddling in the ordinary affairs of their daily lives.

The prohibition era that followed serves as the inspiration for Chicago 1930, which is currently in progress at Spellbound Studios. Described as combining strategy, stealth and action elements, the game reportedly begins with the Mafia controlling large parts of the city. A special police task force has been formed in order to break its hold. At this point, the player's role and objective may seem pretty obvious, but the developers have thrown in a twist by allowing you to play either side. The gangster forces are charged with committing criminal acts such as murders and kidnappings, while the agents of the law must take back the community quarter by quarter by accomplishing missions like investigations. Lead Game Designer and AI Programmer Martin Kuppe provides a great deal more information in this Chicago 1930 Interview, Part 2.


Action Vault: During the course of the game, what assortment of weapons will be available to the player, and to what extent are they accurate in representing the actual arms of the period?

Martin Kuppe: We have four kinds of firearms (Colt .38, Winchester, pump gun and Tommy gun), four kinds of close combat weapon (knuckleduster, baseball bat, baton and switchblade knife) and some other weapons (pineapple grenade, throwing knife, chloroform). The more weapons the player owns, the more weapons he has at his disposition. The weapon characteristics are adapted to the gameplay, not to historical facts.

Action Vault: What were your primary objectives in designing and implementing the enemy NPCs, especially with respect to the artificial intelligence that controls their behavior?

Martin Kuppe: The main goal was to have enemies with realistic and "human-like" behavior. Enemy NPCs react differently when they see player characters, depending on the situation. A gangster drinking his milk shake at the hotel bar will, for example, not react in the same way as a guard in front of a door, a lookout on a street corner, a cop patrolling in the street or a gangster counting money in a back room. Furthermore, the NPCs should fight in an intelligent and cooperative way - look for help, encircle the player characters, hide behind desks, choose optimal shooting position, etc.

Action Vault: Will there be many friendly NPCs, or neutral ones like innocent bystanders? What kinds of roles will they play, and how important will they be overall?

Martin Kuppe: The civilians play an important role in the gameplay. In the police campaign, civilian witnesses are your main source of information. In the Mafia campaign, civilians may become witnesses to your crimes. When a civilian sees one of your characters committing a murder, she becomes a witness - which is indicated by an eye symbol above her head. When you don't take care of the witness, she will tell everything to the police after the mission, and maybe your gangster will go to jail. So, better take care of the witnesses. The simplest way is to kill them - but there is a little problem. Imagine you commit a murder. There is a chambermaid who sees what you did and who runs into the restaurant. Well, you follow her and kill her in public. *bang* But eight of the 20 guests in the restaurant see you - eight more witnesses to kill... *bang, bang, bang...* Now the remaining 12 guests are also witnesses. *Bang, bang, bang, bang...* Oops, the waiter has come out of the kitchen. *Bang*. Just at this moment, five other guests come in...

In the end, you have no more surviving witnesses, and nobody will go to jail - but there is a big heap of dead witnesses in the restaurant. And a big heap of dead bodies in Chicago during the prohibition - everybody knows that this was not the Ku Klux Klan... So, if you kill all the witnesses, the popularity of the Mafia will drop, and you will get less new men. But there is another way to take care of the witnesses. Bribe them! So, if you have some bribe money in your pockets, talk with the chambermaid, give her some bills, and she has seen nothing.

Action Vault: How many missions will there be for each side, and to what degree have you designed them to differ in terms of the gameplay and goals?

Martin Kuppe: There will be overall 20 missions in the game, 10 for each campaign. They provide a wide variety of mission objectives. As a gangster you have, for example, to kill a judge, to destroy the beer reserves of a rival gang, to assassinate enemy gangsters, to kidnap a district attorney or to help Don Falcone out of a police raid. As a fed, you make raids, investigate murders, search for weapons in a secret weapon stock, protect a witness, free hostages, etc.

Action Vault: It seems the game is single-player only? Is this correct, and why did you decide not to have multiplayer?

Martin Kuppe: It's only single-player. The gameplay does not match very well with a multiplayer mode. Initially, we discussed a lot about whether a multiplayer mode is possible, but finally decided to focus on single-player.

Action Vault: What engine are you using, and which of its features are especially important? What do you think the system requirements and recommendations for Chicago 1930 will be?

Martin Kuppe: The engine is based on our own Desperados / Robin Hood engine, but with some improvements. First, the sprites are lighted dynamically. When a character goes into a shadow zone, he gets darker; when he approaches a light, he gets brighter. Second, we have particle effects on all kinds of broken objects - lamps, vases, plants... Furthermore, we have a sprite re-coloring system that allows having an individual sprite for each of the 30 available player characters.

As for the system requirements, the game should run on a PII 300 with 64 MB RAM and a graphics card with 8 MB.

Action Vault: When did the idea for the game arise, and when did development begin? What is the present status, when will it be ready for release, and in which markets are you planning to release it?

Martin Kuppe: In 2002, we discussed what kind of game we could create based on the Robin Hood engine, and finally, we had the idea that a Mafia game would be great. We started work in Q4 2002, and we are just before the alpha version. In Europe, the game will be in stores before Christmas 2003, except if an asteroid crashes down on our company. ;o)

Action Vault: What factors will make Chicago 1930 stand out, and what kind of gamer do you see as your core target market? Approximately what duration of gameplay are you aiming for, and what level of replayability do you anticipate?

Martin Kuppe: As far as I know, Chicago 1930 is the only infiltration tactics game where you can choose between the good guys and the bad guys. Other strong points are the RPG aspect, the background story and the slow motion mode, which transforms messy gunfights into tactical challenges.

The game will have three degrees of difficulty, so occasional players and hardcore gamers can enjoy it. The core target group is gamers who like infiltration and action games such as Commandos, Desperados and Robin Hood.
The duration of gameplay is very difficult to estimate at the current state, but I think it will be around 10 hours of gameplay. The RPG component and the non-linear campaigns will give a strong motivation to replay the game.

Action Vault: Please tell our readers a bit about Spellbound and its history. Where is the studio located and who are the core members of the Chicago 1930 team? What experience do you have in game development?

Martin Kuppe: Spellbound was founded in 1995 and has developed the games Perry Rhodan: Operation Eastside, Airline Tycoon, Desperados and Robin Hood. The studio is located at Kehl, a small German town just at the French border. We just have to cross the Rhine bridge, and we are in Strassbourg. Most of the team members are French.

The key members of our development team are project manager Jean-Michel Stenger, technical director Stéphane Becker, lead programmer Arno Wienholtz, lead level designer Mickael Parisot, and last but not least art director Jean-Marc Haessig - all French people. Oh, and also me. Personally, I am German, but being surrounded by French people, I speak French all day long... But there are over a dozen people working on this project, and every single one plays an important role. Most of them are on the team since the development of Robin Hood or even of Desperados, and some even longer.

Action Vault: Are there any other interesting features or elements you'd like to discuss? Is there anything else you'd like to tell our readers, or something you'd like to ask them?

Martin Kuppe: I just want to say that our artists did (and do) a great job with the level graphics, as they did in Desperados and Robin Hood. The levels are like big paintings, with great ambience and lots of little details, and sometimes (but don't tell this to my boss!) I spend 10 minutes just looking a level background and enjoying the graphics!

Thousands of gamers around the world have enjoyed the graphics and more importantly the gameplay in Desperados, Robin Hood - The Legend of Sherwood and the other titles that have been released to date by Spellbound Studios. We join them in looking forward to the arrival of Chicago 1930 late this year in Europe, and perhaps at a date to be determined in North America as well. In the meantime, we thank Martin Kuppe for answering these questions to complete our two-part interview.
