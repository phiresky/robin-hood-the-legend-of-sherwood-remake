Action Vault > Features > Interviews
Chicago 1930 Interview, Part 1
 August 15 2003


Action Vault: As for the structure of the respective campaigns, how do they begin, and from there, do they continue in a linear progression or is there some degree of player choice?

Martin Kuppe: You choose the next mission on the map of Chicago. Take the Mafia campaign for an example. When you start the game, you see the map of Chicago - eight quarters, corresponding to the eight locations. Five of them are colored brown, which means that they are owned by a rival gang. The remaining three are blue; they are controlled by the authorities. One of the brown quarters to the west is blinking - this is your first mission. You click on it, receive the briefing, compose your team, equip it and start the mission.

After the mission, the quarter is colored in red, which is your color, and two of the adjacent brown quarters are blinking. You can choose where to attack, and so on... This way, the player conquers the city quarter by quarter. The police campaign goes the other way round - the whole map is in red, and you have to re-paint it in blue... Thus we have two non-linear campaigns consisting of 10 missions each (eight missions to conquer the quarters and two additional missions for the storyline).

Action Vault: Since we know the game is squad-based, how many characters does the player actually control at any one time, and when and how are its members selected?

Martin Kuppe: As noted before, in the Mafia campaign the player character is Jack Beretto, the right-hand man of Don Falcone. In the police campaign, the player takes the role of federal agent Edouard Nash, chief of a group of untouchable agents in charge of the fight against the Mafia. The mission team always consists of the main character and up to four other characters that can be chosen freely before each mission. There is a pool of up to 15 different characters per campaign, each one having his own attribute values.

Action Vault: What are these attributes you mentioned, and is there an advancement system that allows characters to improve in them during the course of the game?

Martin Kuppe: There are five attributes - shooting, close combat, throwing, medicine and charisma. Furthermore, the characters may have special bonuses for some actions, for example "Tommy gun +2". The characters can train the attributes by using them - when you use, for example, grenades and throwing knives very often, your throwing attribute gets better.

You can also train your characters in the sports club once you have conquered the quarter with the "sports club" icon. This is an example of the resources placed in the Chicago map. There are the following resources: speakeasies (where you can get new men), armories (where you get access to new weapons and equipment), sports club (to train your men), tribunal (to reduce the time your characters are in jail) and newspaper (to increase popularity).

Action Vault: How does the combat system work, what are its important features, and what is the balance between direct combat and other types of gameplay such as the obvious one, stealth?

Martin Kuppe: The combat system is based on an intuitive point and click interface. You click on a weapon in your inventory, and then click on the adversary to use it. When it is a firearm, we will have a recoil effect on the cursor, depending on the weapon and on the character's shooting ability. Some weapons need a bit more consideration, for example, the pineapple grenade. When you have selected the grenade, you press the left mouse button down to pull the ring, and you release it to throw the grenade. It's all about timing; when you toss it too early, the adversaries will duck and take cover, or even pick up the grenade and throw it back. If you hold it longer, it may explode in your hand, which is not really a good idea. But we have some graphical help for the player. A countdown is displayed over the grenade, and you see the exact trajectory it will take.

As a general remark, you only see what your characters see. When you have seen an enemy and he leaves your 180-degree view area, he slowly fades out. When your characters hear an NPC, this is displayed as a "blip" consisting of concentric circles.

As for the balance, we have maybe 60% stealth, 40% direct combat. In general, you start a mission with stealth tactics, as they are full of adversaries. When you have cleaned up the level a bit, you can dare to use noisy weapons like guns and grenades. Often, the level ends with a big final gunfight.

Action Vault: What kind of pace are you aiming for in the stealth and combat aspects? How will the slow-motion mode work, and will it be possible to pause the action completely to issue orders?

Martin Kuppe: As long as you use stealth tactics, the game is relatively slow. Of course, you sometimes have to react fast; for example, when you see or hear a guard coming around the corner. When it comes to combat, it is, of course, much faster. In fact, in a big gunfight, it may become too fast to handle - therefore, we have the slow motion mode.

With a click on an icon, the game is slowed down by the factor of five. Graphically we have an "old film effect" and motion blur effects on the characters; you can see bullets flying through the air... We hear a heartbeat sound, and the in-game sounds are sampled down. In the slow motion mode, you have enough time to give orders to different characters, react instantly to enemy behavior and so on. Furthermore, you can in this mode assign targets to your characters. One will then shoot at the cop on the left until he is dead, another the cop on the right... The assigned targets are represented by dotted lines; this way, you see which characters have a target and which ones are waiting for new orders.

And, no, we have no pause mode where you give orders - if we would have a pause, nobody would use our nice slow motion mode. ;o)

While Chicago 1930 doesn't have a pause mode, we do - and it's rather useful in instances where developers are willing to provide enough information to span multiple interview segments. As you can see, this is the case here and now. Accordingly, we'll take a break before returning with more from Martin Kuppe on Spellbound's game, which should happen in the not to distant future. And for now, of course, we thank him for what we have learned so far.

Richard Aihoshi - "Jonric"
