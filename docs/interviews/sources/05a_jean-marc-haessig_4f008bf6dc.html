Robin Hood - The Legend of Sherwood Q&A
 October 01, 2002


Action Vault: With respect to the game's overall art direction and its actual graphics, what are the major challenges and obstacles you encountered? Did any real surprises crop up during development, either favorable or unfavorable?

Jean-Marc Haessig: The only obstacles we face relate to resources. These include the game budget, project time, number of team members, as well as the machines we develop our games on and for. There's no limit to the imagination, but reality demands that you respect certain limitations - otherwise you'll get burned! But these are problems every developer faces, and even if we had machines with 20 tetrahertz processors and 120 GB of Ram we'd still be hoping for better!! That's why it's better to speak of challenges rather than obstacles.

We evolved new working methods for this project, and can take pride in the fact that our ETA was so accurate that we didn't need to avail of the usual built-in safety period and were able to announce that the game would be ready for Christmas instead of Easter!

I think that such an event is rare enough to warrant a mention. :)

Action Vault: Since we know the game engine is an updated version of the one you created for Desperados, what considerations led you to use it again for Robin Hood? What modifications and enhancements have been added, especially relating to the graphics portion?

Jean-Marc Haessig: We created the Desperados engine specifically for that game. At the time, we wanted to get away from isometric constraints based on the repetition of blocks and express ourselves as freely as possible with backgrounds made from one block. We invested a lot of time and energy in this engine, but were also aware that it was possible to push it further. For example, despite the freedom we had regarding the positioning of background objects, the isometric perspective used still created a feeling of artificiality. When Wanadoo suggested we use the Desperados engine to produce Robin Hood, we seized the opportunity to try out a few new tricks; for example, we deformed the buildings to give each one an individual look. This makes the graphics look more realistic and brings out the full effect of the imposing medieval buildings!

Action Vault: What type of animation system are you using? What are the main benefits and the key improvements compared to the original implementation in Desperadoes?

Jean-Marc Haessig: The animation system is based on sprites. We developed a tool which allows us to very quickly integrate renderings and process them image by image in order to control the speed and fluidity of movements with a minimum of images. Using sprites also allows for the same control over detail as over the background - to the very pixel - which seemed to us the best means of creating a coherent whole. Because of this - and because we increased the size by 125% - the characters in Robin Hood are very detailed, with realistic shapes and textures.

Action Vault: What are the main features of the graphics engine? To what extent will it take advantage of the features offered by the latest generation of video cards? What resolutions are supported? To what extent can the player control the camera? What CPU and what type of video card will be required to see the game at its best?

Jean-Marc Haessig: Robin Hood's graphics engine is based on data compression - which is very slow - and its subsequent decompression and display - which is very fast. Unfortunately, we can't take advantage of the features offered by the latest cards as these are increasingly dedicated to real-time 3D graphics. This is highly regrettable given what can be done in 2D. No 3D card would be able to reproduce graphics as richly detailed as Robin Hood's.
The engine supports resolutions of 640x480, 800x600 and 1024x768. It's possible to zoom from 50% to 200%. The ideal system spec would be a Pentium III with 128 MB of RAM and a resolution of 800x600. On slower machines, players can set their resolution to 640x480 and, for example, turn off alpha blits - which would only influence enemies' fields of vision but would have no effect on the backgrounds and characters.

Action Vault: What aspects of the art direction and graphics in Robin Hood are you most pleased with, and why?

Jean-Marc Haessig: Once again, our team has outdone themselves and the final product is much better than we initially hoped. It's a bit like when you order a coffee and get a nice chocolate with it without having to ask for it. Well, with our team, you get a blueberry muffin as well. :) I think the fact that a game is a labor of love can felt by people playing it. Synergy in a team is a wonderful thing and we hope that people will have as much fun playing our game as we had creating it!

Action Vault: Is there anything else you think our readers might find interesting on the subject of the game's art direction, or possibly something you'd like to ask them?

Jean-Marc Haessig: Once you've got a taste for chocolate with your coffee, you'll be disappointed if you don't get any... So the moral of the story is forget about the chocolate and you'll be much happier! ;)

Whether they're compared to chocolate, blueberry muffins or any other treats, the visuals in Robin Hood - The Legend of Sherwood amply demonstrate the talents of the Spellbound Studios art team. We certainly look forward to seeing more in the coming weeks and also in the full game, which is projected for release within a fairly short time. And naturally, thanks to Jean-Marc Haessig for his interesting and informative answers to our questions on the topic.
